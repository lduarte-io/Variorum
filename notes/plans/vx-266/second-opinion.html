The short version

The proposal's design is right, and I'd build it. Working from first principles before reading it, I reached the same core:

  • the mode is recorded, not guessed;
  • opening a copy never lowers protection;
  • older keys travel forward with the library;
  • Keychain items are kept per key, not per library;
  • one function decides and one component writes;
  • long acts are written down outside the library file;
  • the library knows its account;
  • a generated scenario test is the proof.

Two independent designs converging is good evidence the shape is sound.

But the most important thing I found isn't in the proposal, and it's a gap in Castle's own promise.

  • Castle promises that someone who knows your Mac's login password can't read your library. For the library itself that holds.
  • For every copy made before you entered Castle — backups, older libraries, Time Machine — it very likely doesn't. The iCloud key that opens those copies is never deleted, and Apple's Keychain Access app can reveal it with nothing but the login password.
  • I've confirmed this from Keychain Access's entitlements on this Mac and Apple's documentation, but not yet on Variorum's own item. One two-minute check by you settles it (below).
  • Today's Castle screen says those copies "still open through your Apple account". It never says "and on this Mac, for anyone who knows its login password".
  • Nothing in the proposal changes this. Neither does my "never open without asking" rule, because someone with the key in hand doesn't need Variorum at all.

The fix is the one structural change I'd add: retire the Apple key when a library enters Castle (§3).

  • Each library, and each sync account, gets its own Apple key, named by its own id.
  • When a library enters Castle, its key is deleted. iCloud Keychain then removes it from every device, with no recovery.
  • At that moment, every copy Variorum ever made of the library stops opening through the Apple account — anywhere.
  • For stores that exist today, yours included, a one-time question offers to retire today's shared key in the same way.

That replaces the "delete your old backups?" offer with two plain rules:

In Castle, your Apple account opens nothing of this library. In Standard, it opens your library and every copy Variorum made of it.

The real cost is the way back. Your original design kept pre-Castle copies openable through the Apple account deliberately, so that losing your passphrase, words and Touch ID would still leave you your older writing. That choice is yours to remake.

Four more changes to the proposal, in order of weight:

  1. Plans and protection settings must be sealed, not just stored.
    • A plan file planted beside the library could make the app finish a "leave Castle" you never chose.
    • An edited "Always ask" setting could make the app create a Touch ID item that a newly enrolled finger opens.
    • Keychain Access can edit keychain items as well as files, so sealing with a key derived from the library's key is the only protection. (§2.3)
  2. Backups must belong to a library and a Mac. Today, retention counts every Variorum backup in a folder. The iCloud Drive offer opens its picker at iCloud Drive's root, so your Pro and your Air, if both accept it, share one folder and prune each other's history. (§2.4)
  3. Lock the key history under the library's current key, not a separate history key.
    • This removes a key, and four states in which the history is only partly locked.
    • It makes every restore of your own library work with nothing typed.
    • It re-accepts cold read 1's blocker as a stated trade: after you leave Castle, your Apple account opens the copies Variorum kept while you were in Castle. For a synced library, today's protection after leaving already stops at "copies from before your last key replacement". (§2.1)
  4. A library this Mac remembers in Castle never opens without asking, whatever file sits in its place. Without this, someone with the login password can swap an older backup into place and open a local-only Castle library with no Touch ID. It's defence in depth: it closes the no-tools route; §3 closes the rest. (§2.2)

You asked what already-built things are holding the design back. In order:

  1. The shared, never-deleted Apple key. It's why Castle can't cover older copies, why the delete-your-backups offer exists, and why the login password reaches pre-Castle writing.
  2. The rule that the mode is "derived, never a setting". It meant to prevent drift, but everything it reads is changed as a side effect elsewhere, so it drifts anyway.
  3. Keychain items named by library rather than by key.
  4. Several key files beside a library, with "current" meaning "most recently modified".
  5. Crash markers kept inside the library file.
  6. "Enrolled" meaning "sync keys are on this Mac", with new rows stamped by whoever happens to be signed in.
  7. Trusting anything stored on the Mac — files or keychain items — to make a decision before unlock.

§4 has each with what to do. Your decisions are in §7, each with my recommendation. One check needed your hands (done 2026-10-13 — confirmed on the Pro and the Air):

  1. Open Keychain Access and search for apple-key.
  2. Open the item whose Account is Variorum-dev-apple-key (the dev store's). It's probably listed as io.lduarte.Variorum.apple-key. If only Variorum-apple-key, your real store's, is there, use that.
  3. Tick Show password and enter your login password.

If a value appears, the gap in the short version is confirmed; don't copy it anywhere. If the item isn't listed, or nothing is revealed, tell me — that's good news, and it lowers §3's urgency. About two minutes.

2. What I would change in the proposal

2.1 The key history: lock it under the current library key, and say plainly what leaving Castle means

What the proposal does (its §3.2). A history key H opens the list of the library's older keys:

  • H is wrapped under your passphrase and words only.
  • It is kept in the Touch ID item while you're in Castle, and never kept on the device in Standard.
  • It is replaced at every re-key, re-locking the whole history each time.

It exists because cold read 1's one blocker, B1, showed that a history reachable through the account master would, after leaving Castle, hand Castle-era keys to anyone in your Apple account.

What H costs. A fourth kind of key, and four states where the history is only partly locked. The reviewer confirmed all four.

  • A passphrase changed on another device. A Standard or Always-ask device's key file still holds H under the old passphrase. When it later follows a key replacement, you type the new one, which can't open H. The history stops there unless you also type the words.
  • A follow takes one secret. The new H gets one wrap; the other "lands the next time that secret is typed here".
  • Standard keeps no H on the device. Every restore of an older-key backup asks for your passphrase or words, even on your own Mac with the library open.
  • H can only be made when a secret is typed. The migration has to ask, and backups made before then carry a history nothing opens.

The alternative. Wrap every older key under the library's current key, in the key file and in every backup's frozen copy of it.

  • Whatever opens the library — Touch ID, the silent item, the passphrase, the words, the master, or the Apple key in Standard — opens its past.
  • A re-key re-wraps the history with keys already in hand, so nothing is ever typed for the history's sake and no pending state exists.
  • The chain runs backwards only, so entering Castle is unaffected: nothing Apple-reachable leads to anything written after the switch. Both cold reads confirm this.

What it gives up — this re-accepts cold read 1's blocker B1, as a stated trade. Exactly:

  1. After any road into Standard — Use Standard, joining or moving to a Standard account, or a follow into Standard — the Apple account reaches the current key and, through the history, keys retired while the library was in Castle. So Castle-era copies under a key you replaced before leaving become Apple-openable.
  2. In Standard, copies from the exposed era (made while no Apple copy had landed) become Apple-openable once the Apple key lands. That is Standard working as designed: they were Standard copies whose Apple copy never landed. A Standard Replace All Keys changes nothing here, because its older copies carry Apple wraps of their own (decision 30).

What today's protection after leaving actually is. Leaving Castle gives the Apple account the key you leave with. For a synced library it also gives it the master: every key file carries a master copy, and leaving adds an Apple copy of the master to the vault.

So today, and under the proposal's H, the Castle-era copies that stay sealed after leaving are only those under keys retired before you left — in practice, copies from before your last Replace All Keys. That boundary is invisible to users. Most people never replace their keys, so for them H protects nothing.

The honest sentence for the leave screen under my version: "Leaving Castle gives your Apple account a key to your library and to every copy Variorum keeps of it." Under §3, the key you leave with is a fresh one, and the sentence is the same.

If you want Castle-era copies to stay sealed after leaving, neither H nor my version does it fully. That needs a re-key at leaving, plus a full master rotation for synced libraries. I don't recommend it: leaving Castle is a deliberate choice to trust your Apple account with the library again.

Recommendation: history under the current key, with the honest leave sentence. It's your call, because it re-accepts a reviewed blocker.

2.2 A library this Mac remembers in Castle never opens without asking

Withdrawn after your D3 ruling (2026-10-13). Once entering Castle deletes the Apple key, every copy of a Castle library — wherever it is — opens only with Touch ID (through the history), the passphrase or the twelve words. So this rule has nothing left to stop.

  • The one leftover case: a store whose owner declines the one-time question about today's shared key. They chose that, with the warning.
  • Planted files and edited settings are §2.3's job (sealing), not this rule's.
  • Your question — the person who entered Castle, forgot everything, lost their writing and starts again. Nothing stops them. Start a New Library makes a new library with its own key and nothing of the old one. The old library's copies stay unopenable, which is Castle's promise, said at the door. Variorum can later offer to clear the old library's leftovers (its key item, its backups) once you confirm you can't open them.

The original text follows, for the record.

The gap. Two places in the proposal accept a silent open first:

  • §3.4: "If what this device remembers says the library is in Castle but a road opened it without asking … the reconciler brings it into Castle straight away and says so."
  • §4: "Someone puts an older, Apple-openable copy where your library was. It opens through the Apple road, as it would today; after unlock … the reconciler brings it into Castle at once."

"Brings it into Castle" re-encrypts under the current key, which needs Touch ID or your passphrase. If the person at the keyboard isn't you, they have read the copy by then.

The attack, traced at HEAD for a local-only Castle library (the reviewer confirmed it end to end):

  1. Someone who knows the Mac's login password moves a pre-Castle backup pair into the library's place, with Finder.
  2. They open Variorum. The route reads the old key file, sees an Apple copy and no silent item, and derives Standard (StoreProtection.swift:101-109).
  3. It opens silently through the Apple key (AppBootstrap.swift:350-357). Today the key is then also re-saved silently (:548-556).

A synced Castle library is already defended at this route. Its sync keys are on the Mac, so the reading is "indeterminate", the custody shape says Castle, and the Castle lobby appears (StoreLaunchGate.swift:408, StoreProtection.swift:91-93, 113-116).

The fix:

  • The never-lower rule is keyed by library ID, through what this device remembers, not by what sits at the location.
  • Any file of a library this Mac remembers in Castle opens only after Touch ID or the passphrase — at the library's place, picked in Open Library, or picked from a fresh library after the container was deleted. Cold read m19 already establishes that the keychain survives that deletion.
  • After unlock, the history reaches the older key, and the copy is re-encrypted under the current key before anything shows.
  • A Forget this library on this Mac door lets you abandon a library deliberately. It needs Touch ID or the passphrase, because forgetting lowers protection.

What it doesn't stop. Someone who reads the Apple key out of Keychain Access, then decrypts a backup with a short script, never touches Variorum. They can also delete the "remembered" record in Keychain Access. So this rule closes the no-tools route, and §3 closes the rest. It's still worth having, because it makes Variorum's own behaviour honest.

Robustness to go with it: what this device remembers keeps a mirror of the current key file's wrapped keys and history, so a rolled-back or deleted key file never strands the library on this Mac. The cost is a second copy to keep in step. The executor writes the key file and its mirror in one act, and the mirror is checked against the key file at every ready.

2.3 Plans and protection settings are sealed

The gap. Plans live beside the library, hold facts but no secrets, and the reconciler resumes them after unlock (the proposal's §3.5). Anything on the Mac can be written by someone with the login password. That includes keychain items: Keychain Access holds a wildcard keychain entitlement and can presumably edit what it lists (Apple facts 1–2).

  • A planted plan reading Leave Castle, next step: add the Apple copy would be resumed at your next unlock. The app would add the Apple wrap and move the key to the silent item: you'd be in Standard without having chosen it. The proposal's "a plan whose world doesn't match is ignored as stale" doesn't stop it, because the library ID and key fingerprints are public in every key file. The reviewer confirmed this against the proposal.
  • A protection setting works the same way. Today the adoption (LibraryOpenModel.swift:538), the migration and the switch all write the Touch ID item from a stored setting. Flip "Always ask" to Touch ID, enroll a finger, and wait for your next passphrase unlock: the repair builds a Touch ID item that the new finger opens. The same goes for setting the idle lock to "never".

The fix:

  • Sealing. Every plan, and every stored setting that could weaken protection (the gate style, the idle lock), is sealed with a key derived from the library key in hand when it is written, and names that key's fingerprint.
  • Verifying. After unlock, the executor verifies the seal with that key, reaching it through the history if the plan's own act has re-keyed the library since. An Always-ask device verifies after the typed unlock and before any step runs.
  • On a bad seal: an unsealed or mismatched plan is ignored and reported, and an unsealed setting reads as the stricter value. A plan that lowers protection also carries the record of the consent you gave at its door.
  • Before unlock, nothing on the Mac decides anything. The lobby needn't know which key is current. It tries every road this device has for the library, and each road verifies itself, because a key that doesn't open the file opens nothing. That replaces the proposal's "the lobby reads the plan" (its m6(e) answer) and my draft's key-file field, which the reviewer rightly flagged.

2.4 Backups belong to a library and a Mac

The hazard, traced by my helper and confirmed by the reviewer:

  • StoreBackup.existingBackups (StoreBackup.swift:704-716) lists every Variorum-named snapshot in a folder.
  • prune(keep:) (:609-615) and the far folder's 14-daily-plus-4-weekly rule (BackupRetention.swift:87-118) count them all, whichever library or Mac made them.
  • The iCloud Drive offer opens its picker at iCloud Drive's root (ICloudDriveOffer.swift:101-106), and backups land in <pick>/Variorum/Backups.

So two Macs on one Apple account that both accept the offer share a folder. Each Mac's retention deletes the other's older backups, and a busy Mac can push a quiet Mac's history out entirely. That's your Pro and your Air.

The fix: the proposal's one catalog attributes every copy to its library (the pair's library ID) and to the Mac that made it. Retention acts only on this Mac's copies of this library. A per-library subfolder inside the picked folder is the simple way. Existing shared folders migrate by attribution, never by deletion.

The Castle offer may still list another Mac's copies of a synced library — they hold the same account's writing — but it must say whose they are. Under §3 the offer mostly disappears.

Other hazards from the map that the proposal settles in substance but doesn't list; each needs a red-on-old pin in its phase:

  • The Apple arm can change a library's identity. ensuredEnvelope sets aside a key file that doesn't match and starts a new library ID (ProtectionController.swift:1129-1142).
  • A crash in adoption can leave a library with no key file. Adoption lands the library before its key file (LibraryAdoption.swift:295-304), so a crash between the two leaves ciphertext and no recovery road. Pair and folder key-file writes are also best-effort try?.
  • The restore list promises roads it never checks. "Your current passphrase (online)" is shown for copies made before the master was replaced, which it can't open (LibraryOpenCopy.swift:114).
  • The lock-time copy can carry the wrong name. It is named from the located key file, not from the key its bytes are under (StoreBackup.swift:648-655).
  • Resume Sync can hide a hold that still stands. It clears the banner even when removing the marker failed (RestoreHoldModel.swift:85-89).
  • Leftovers pile up. .stale-* key files are never read or removed, and older libraries and safety copies only ever go through the Castle offer. Give older libraries a retention rule of their own.

2.5 Optional: secrets in custody, everything else in what the device remembers

The proposal keeps the device identity inside the sync-keys item, which is why Castle needs an "identity-only" silent item beside the Touch ID one. Moving the identity into what the device remembers removes that item. Then custody holds only secrets, and everything non-secret sits in one record.

This reverses the proposal's answer to cold read 1's m9, so it isn't free. It needs a migration leg that carries the existing identity, or every device mints a new one and leaves an old cursor holding the purge horizon. It also needs a guard against cloned identities: iOS Quick Start has been seen to carry this-device-only items to a new phone (Apple fact 10), so re-mint the identity when the hardware changes. Worth doing if the open-road phase has room; not required.

3. The structural change: retire the Apple key when a library enters Castle

Today:

  • There is one Apple key per Apple account, per store, named by the store alone (AppleKeyCustody.swift:135). Every library and every Variorum account on that Apple account shares it, and it is "never deleted in production" (:189-190).
  • Entering Castle removes the copies under it from the live key file and the vault. Every older key file keeps its copy, so every backup, older library, Time Machine snapshot and iCloud Drive pair made before Castle stays openable through the Apple account.
  • The Apple account here is broader than it sounds. It includes anyone who can add a device to your iCloud Keychain: the Apple account plus any trusted device's passcode, a recovery contact or a recovery key (Apple fact 4). It also very likely includes anyone who knows this Mac's login password, through Keychain Access (fact 1).
  • That's why the Castle offer exists. VX-203 and VX-208 grew it in VX-201's run, and VX-210, VX-265, VX-263's offer wording and part of VX-209 follow from it.
  • It's also why the VX-191 audit's server residual exists: the server's own backups of the old vault row, read with the Apple key, still open pre-Castle writing.

The change:

  1. Apple keys named by their own id. Each local-only library and each sync account gets its own Apple key, named by its id: <scope>-apple-key-<id>, with the store variant kept. The vault already records which key its Apple copy is under (apple_key_id), and so does a library's key file (appleKeyID).
  2. One minter. Only the device that makes an account or library Standard mints its key: sync setup in Standard, leaving Castle, a fresh local library. Any other device that can't read that key waits. Not yet arrived, iCloud Keychain off, and another Apple ID all look the same to it; there's no API to tell them apart (fact 6). A device on another Apple ID may use a key of its own, under its own name, for its own file only (corner 34's road). Two devices therefore never mint the same item. The same-item conflict that orphaned the vault's copy before the pre-13x ladder's F2/F3 fixes can't happen by construction.
    • This rebuilds reconcileAppleKey's mint-and-wait rule (ProtectionController.swift:1600-1690), so it reopens that settled ground. It gets its own convergence pins: the leave race, the slow arrival, the other Apple ID.
  3. Retire on entering Castle. Once the switch has committed, the account's or library's Apple key is deleted, and so is every device key made for it; each device deletes its own when it follows. Deletes of synchronizable items reach every device, with no "recently deleted" for app items (fact 3).
    • The Castle repair is "delete it whenever it's seen". A key deleted while this Mac's iCloud Keychain was off can come back when sync returns, and OS updates have silently turned sync back on (fact 6).
  4. Leaving Castle mints a fresh key. The other devices wait for it. Their keys move to the silent item meanwhile, and their Apple copy lands when the key arrives — the existing "waiting for iCloud Keychain" fact, not a lockout.
  5. Stores that exist today, yours included.
    • A Standard store keeps today's shared key until it enters Castle. That steady state is already guarded: nothing deletes the shared key, so nothing races.
    • When such a store enters Castle — or, once, for a store already in Castle (yours) — Variorum asks whether to retire the old shared key.
    • The question says what that means: "Older copies of this library made before Castle — backups, older libraries, Time Machine — stop opening through your Apple account. Any other device signed into this Apple account that keeps a Variorum library in Standard will keep opening it, but its own older copies will need your passphrase or twelve words."
    • For you, with every library in Castle, it costs nothing current. Your pre-Castle copies (VX-99 counted 13 Apple-openable pairs on your Pro on 8 October) stop opening through the Apple account. You can still open them with Touch ID or your passphrase, through the history.

What it buys:

  • Castle covers every copy Variorum ever made of the library, the moment the key is retired: iCloud Drive, Time Machine, copies you carried elsewhere, and the server's own backups. The exception is anything someone already opened before then; no design can recall that.
  • The login password stops reaching pre-Castle writing on this Mac, through Variorum or around it.
  • The Castle offer has nothing left to ask about Apple reach. No "delete your backups?", and no background job bringing backups into Castle. What remains is VX-208's Markdown-exports line, and listing older libraries of other libraries.
  • Two plain rules replace a paragraph of caveats (the short version).
  • Accounts stop sharing a key, so corner 14's "each account's copies are its own" becomes literal.

What it costs:

  1. The way back. In Castle, losing your passphrase, your words and Touch ID loses the library and its older backups. Today the pre-Castle backups would still open through your Apple account. Your original design kept them deliberately, "as a limit and a way back". This is the trade, and it's yours.
  2. More Apple-key events. A mint at every leave, a delete at every entry, and the follower-wait rule above. Leaves and entries are rare, deliberate acts.
  3. Other devices wait after a leave. After leaving Castle on one device, the others open normally but carry no Apple copy until the new key arrives through iCloud Keychain.
  4. It doesn't replace the re-key at entry. Re-keying the library and rotating the account keys still defend against someone who took a key while you were in Standard. This change adds protection against someone who reaches the Apple key after you enter.

Recommendation: adopt it, with the one-time question for existing stores, unless the way back matters more to you than a Castle that covers everything. Pre-launch is the cheap moment: everyone who starts after it ships never has a shared key at all.

Appendix A — the cases, walked under my version

VX-209, local only. You restore a pre-Castle backup.

  1. This Mac remembers the library in Castle, so the open asks for Touch ID first.
  2. The history reaches the backup's key, and the copy is re-encrypted under the current key straight into place.
  3. It stays in Castle. The Touch ID item is never touched, because it belongs to the current key.

Bringing the Castle library back moves nothing. Under D3, the backup's own Apple copy is already dead.

VX-209, synced. The same; then Resume Sync catches up and the vault confirms Castle.

W-1 / VX-263. Entering Castle from the exposed state puts the old key into the new key's history. Every earlier backup opens with whatever opens the library now. The leftover key file goes, so VX-213 and VX-263 are both satisfied.

A passphrase changed on the Air, then a key replacement the Pro follows. The Pro follows by typing the new passphrase through the vault, and re-wraps its history with the library key it already holds. Nothing is lost. (Under the history-key design, a Standard Pro's history key is locked under the old passphrase, so the history stops at this follow.)

Leaving Castle after a Replace All Keys.

  • Under my version, the Apple account reaches the key you leave with and its history, so Castle-era backups open through the Apple account. The leave screen said so.
  • Under the proposal, backups under the replaced key stay sealed; those under the key you leave with don't.
  • Under D3, the key you leave with is wrapped under a freshly minted Apple key, and the same rule as mine holds.

Someone with your login password, on a Mac in Castle.

  • Without D3: they can very likely read the shared Apple key in Keychain Access and open every pre-Castle backup with a script. D4 only stops the version that uses Variorum.
  • With D3: the key is gone, so there's nothing to read. Touch ID items resist Keychain Access, and the library itself stays closed.

A planted plan or an edited setting. Not sealed with the library's key, so the plan is ignored and reported, and the setting reads as the stricter value (D5).

Pro and Air sharing an iCloud Drive backup folder. Each Mac prunes only its own copies of its own library, and the offer names whose copies it lists (D6).

A new Mac, from the backup folder only.

  1. Open the newest pair with your passphrase or words.
  2. Its history opens every older pair.
  3. In Standard, the account's Apple key, arriving through iCloud Keychain, opens them with nothing typed.
  4. Under D3, Castle-era pairs open only with your secrets, which is the point.

An Apple account change on this Mac (Standard).

  • The silent item keeps opening the library.
  • Under D3, this Mac can't read the account's key, so it waits. For its own file it may use a key of its own, under the new Apple account.
  • Older copies open through the history.

A follower that reconciles before a freshly minted key arrives (D3). It waits; it never mints a key under the vault's id. Its keys sit in the silent item meanwhile, and the Apple copy lands when the key arrives. No same-item conflict can occur.
