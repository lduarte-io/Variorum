Castle & Standard

The settled shape, revised 2026-10-14 — what each mode means, what every switch does to every key, and why a copy can never betray you. A companion page; the binding text is notes/plans/vx-266-castle-backups-prelaunch.md and the acceptance artifact is castle-target-key-map.html.

The rules.

In Castle, only you open your library and every copy Variorum made of it: your finger, your passphrase, or your twelve words — with the stated exceptions the target map lists. In Standard, your Apple account opens it, through iCloud Keychain when it's on — and so can anyone who signs in to this Mac. In Castle = safe. Out of Castle = not safe.

Leaving Castle gives your Apple account the library from then on — the library itself, with everything you wrote in it, and every copy made after you leave. No copy made before you left opens through it: those open with the passphrase or the twelve words you had on the day you left, and leaving never makes new words, so that day's paper keeps working. Your other devices each follow with one secret typed, so their own copies from before open with the words, and with the passphrase when it was the one you typed there to follow; each copy's Opens with line says which, and from which day.

The one mechanism under everything: a library gets a fresh key whenever it crosses a boundary — into Castle, out of Castle, leaving an account, landing on another Mac. What a key could open before a boundary, it can never open after. Your own past rides forward: fully into Castle; out of Castle it waits in the anchor, behind the passphrase and words you had the day you left.

Only you can open anything. You're about to trade that for convenience — knowingly.

It is locked once, here, and never re-locked: it keeps today's secrets, and every later re-key and backup carries it unchanged. This is where your past waits.

3New sync keys — the vault marked as leaving

Synced only: a new master and sync key; your writing re-encrypted; the vault rewritten with the new master under your passphrase and your words. The rotation's first write also names the account's fresh Apple key — minted at your press — in the vault, with no wrap: the mark that this rotation is a leave. Past that write a crash resumes, never rolls back. Every device reads the vault's mode in three states: a wrap means Standard; a key named with no wrap, leaving Castle, in progress; neither, Castle.

Sync keys are held by what they can reach: until the previous sync key leaves the vault, the new keys and the previous one are held the Castle way — the account's Touch ID sync item, made at your press, or session memory with Always ask — so nothing from the Castle era is ever within the login password's reach.

4One write completes the leave

Synced only: one write adds the account's Apple wrap of the new master, under the key the vault names, and closes the previous sync key's window in the same write — so nothing from the Castle era ever sits under anything Apple can reach. Only a device that can read that named key makes it: this Mac, or another of your devices once iCloud Keychain carries the key there. The others re-encrypt, but never clear the previous key while a key stands named with no wrap; if an older build clears it first, the leave still reads as in progress, and the completing write is just the wrap. Then the new sync keys move to a silent item, and the Touch ID item goes.

If this Mac never finishes, another of your devices finishes it at a press that names it — “Finish leaving Castle, started on [device] on [day]” — waiting for the named key, and minting a fresh one only if it never arrives. 7Your other devices follow — one typed secret each

There is no silent way out of Castle. Each device asks for your passphrase or words once, for the new master. A device that sees the rotation still running — the previous sync key still in the vault, or the account's key named with no wrap — holds its re-key until the vault reads Standard or Castle, and keeps the keys it takes the Castle way while the previous sync key stands. Then it writes its own anchor first, under the secret it just typed — that secret opened the vault, so it is current — and under its outgoing Castle key, with that key's own locks from its key file copied beside it. The one secret typed does both jobs, and nothing re-locks the anchor after it's written. So its anchor opens with the secret typed at its follow and with whatever opened that device that day; only a device that never had the passphrase typed has an anchor that opens with the words alone, and its copies' Opens with line says the twelve words. (After an older build's leave, which never changed the master, the master's road is left out.) Then it re-keys to a Standard-born key of its own.

The four boundaries

Same rule at every one: a fresh key, and the old key's reach ends at the line.

INTO CASTLE
Everything folds in.

A fresh Castle-born key; every older key and any waiting anchor joins its history (an anchor the secrets you type can't open waits beside it, unchanged). Your whole past opens with Touch ID, your passphrase or your words — and with nothing else, anywhere, ever again.

OUT OF CASTLE
Nothing crosses.

A fresh Standard-born key with an empty history. The past — Castle keys and pre-Castle keys alike — waits in the anchor, behind the passphrase and words you had that day, never re-locked. Re-entering folds it back in whole.

LEAVING AN ACCOUNT
Both modes re-key.

The account you leave opens only the copies from its Standard days — until it replaces its keys or enters Castle — and never anything you write after. Any anchor rides along unchanged. In Standard the switch needs nothing remembered: the silent item and this Mac's Apple key carry it, and a forgotten passphrase is simply reset at the door.

LANDING ON ANOTHER MAC
A copy becomes its own library.

A sent library lands under a fresh key with an empty history: unlock with one secret, then type the other — or mint a new one, since the one you typed proved it's yours. No two Macs ever share a key — except a library sent before this change, until either crosses a line — so sending a copy never ties your safety to someone else's choices. (A Standard copy on the same Apple account can land with nothing typed; it lands on the Apple road, secrets offered — declined, it opens through that Mac and its Apple account only.)

The anchor, in one picture

In Castletoday's key opens the history → every older key → every older copy. One chain, backwards only. An old key never opens anything newer.
At the leavethe chain is lifted whole into the anchor and sealed under that day's P and W (on a Mac that followed, under the secret typed at its follow and its own locks for the outgoing key). The new Standard key starts a new, empty chain.
In Standard, afterApple opens the new chain only. The anchor answers to the passphrase or words you had the day you left — it keeps the secrets of its day, and nothing re-seals it. Leaving makes no new words, so that day's paper keeps working.

The one thing to keep: that day's paper. Every door that replaces a secret while an anchor exists says what still opens the copies from before the leave. New words, Replace All Keys, a leave from an account and sync setup's new words say “Copies from before 12 October still open with the words you're replacing — keep that paper.” Change passphrase says “Copies from before 12 October still open with the passphrase you're replacing, or that day's words.” Standard's reset road for a forgotten passphrase says “Copies from before 12 October now open with that day's twelve words — keep that paper.” A Use Castle that can't fold an anchor in says which secrets, of which day, still open the copies it holds. Change both your passphrase and your words, and those copies need the old ones; each copy's Opens with line names the day.

What got simpler

Each of these was an open question with a long, conditional answer. The boundary rule closed them.

"Which mode is this library in?" derived from five separate inputs, each changed as a side effect by something else; one ambiguous state could record the wrong mode. written in the record — and structurally, the mode is just the kind of the current key. A key is born Castle or Standard and never changes kind, so there is nothing to derive.
"Which key file and Keychain slot count?" several key files per folder with newest-modified winning, and one Keychain slot shared by every copy of a library — restoring a backup silently overwrote the live key. one key file; one item per key. A restore never evicts anything, and opening a copy can never lower the mode.
"What opens an old backup?" each copy kept only its own day's keys; a library with no passphrase could lose everything with the Mac. the history: whatever opens your library opens everything older, nothing more typed — except, after a leave from Castle, what came before it, which the anchor holds under that day's secrets.
"What does leaving Castle expose?" three rulings in a row wrestled with which eras re-open to Apple, and the answer needed era bookkeeping on every key. the anchor: out of Castle, nothing crosses; back in, everything folds back — or waits beside the history, unchanged, if the secrets you type then can't open it. One object instead of a filtering rule.
"What does AirDropping a library share?" both Macs shared one key and its whole history forever — a standing exception, a hand-off ledger, caveats at Send Library, and the round-2 reviews' worst finding. a landing mints a fresh key with an empty history. A sent copy is just a copy: no two Macs ever share a key — except a library sent before this change, until either crosses a line — and the hand-off ledger and the caveats are gone.
"Can the account I left still read my library?" yes, silently — the old account's master kept opening the library's current key through old backups, including everything written after leaving. leaving re-keys in both modes: the old account opens only copies from its synced Standard days, until it replaces its keys or enters Castle — never anything after.
"Which Apple key wraps what?" one shared Apple key for every library and every account, never deleted — the root of Castle's gap, the "delete your old backups?" offer, and a special case for devices joining old accounts. one key per Mac per library, one per account for the vault's master only. Entering Castle deletes every key of this Mac's own for this library, by its id; the special case no longer exists.
"Is a half-finished switch dangerous?" crash markers lived inside the library file, rode into backups, and one stuck marker could refuse sync forever. sealed plans named by the record; a missing plan never blocks; copies pause around the swap, so every backup is whole.
"Can something on the Mac flip my protection?" the gate style, the idle lock and the scheduled export sat where any login-password holder could edit them, and pre-unlock code decided from what it found on disk. before unlock, nothing decides — the lobby offers only roads that prove themselves by opening the file. Weakening settings live in two sealed homes, newer wins, and a Mac finds its own section by its hardware; no repair ever makes a Touch ID item; the plaintext export turns off on the way into Castle.
"Who owns new writing?" whoever happened to be signed in stamped the rows, and "enrolled" had five meanings. the record names the account; writing belongs to the library's account. Signing in or out changes nothing.

The three honest sentences

What remains isn't complexity — it's truth the design states instead of hiding.

Leaving Castle opens the library you keep. Everything you wrote in Castle is still in it. The anchor protects your old copies — nothing can make the live library private again except going back into Castle.
A copy's locks are frozen the day it's made, and so are the anchor's. Your current passphrase and words follow the library — but a copy, or an anchor, left behind in an era whose secrets you discarded answers only to the secrets of its day. Every copy says what opens it, in one line, wherever it's listed.
Variorum can't see iCloud Keychain. Whether the Apple road actually reached iCloud is Apple's to know. The passphrase and the words are the durable roads, in both modes.
