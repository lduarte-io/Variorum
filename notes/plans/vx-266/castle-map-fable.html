Castle & Standard

The settled shape, 2026-10-13 — what each mode means, what every switch does to every key, and why a copy can never betray you. A companion page; the binding text is notes/plans/vx-266-castle-backups-prelaunch.md and the acceptance artifact is castle-target-key-map.html.

Two rules.

In Castle, only you open your library and every copy of it: your finger, your passphrase, or your twelve words. In Standard, your Apple account opens it — and so can anyone who signs in to this Mac. In Castle = safe. Out of Castle = not safe.

The one mechanism under everything: a library gets a fresh key whenever it crosses a boundary — into Castle, out of Castle, leaving an account, landing on another Mac. What a key could open before a boundary, it can never open after. Your own past rides forward: fully into Castle; out of Castle it waits in the anchor, behind your passphrase and words.

The cast

The history & the family treeThe history: older keys, each locked under today's key — your roads to old copies. The tree: a plain list of every key the library ever had, so copies are recognized without opening anything.

The four boundaries

Same rule at every one: a fresh key, and the old key's reach ends at the line.

INTO CASTLE
Everything folds in.

A fresh Castle-born key; every older key and any waiting anchor joins its history. Your whole past opens with Touch ID, your passphrase or your words — and with nothing else, anywhere, ever again.

OUT OF CASTLE
Nothing crosses.

A fresh Standard-born key with an empty history. The past — Castle keys and pre-Castle keys alike — waits in the anchor, behind your passphrase and words only. Re-entering folds it back in whole.

LEAVING AN ACCOUNT
Both modes re-key.

The account you leave can open the copies made while it synced — never anything you write after. In Standard the switch needs nothing remembered: the silent item and this Mac's Apple key carry it, and a forgotten passphrase is simply reset at the door.

LANDING ON ANOTHER MAC
A copy becomes its own library.

A sent library lands under a fresh key with an empty history: unlock with one secret, then type the other — or mint a new one, since the one you typed proved it's yours. No two Macs ever share a key, so sending a copy never ties your safety to someone else's choices. (A Standard copy on the same Apple account can land with nothing typed; it lands on the Apple road, secrets offered.)

The anchor, in one picture

In Castletoday's key opens the history → every older key → every older copy. One chain, backwards only. An old key never opens anything newer.
At the leavethe chain is lifted whole into the anchor and sealed under P and W. The new Standard key starts a new, empty chain.
In Standard, afterApple opens the new chain only. The anchor answers to your passphrase or words — and your passphrase and words follow you: change them, and the doors re-seal the anchor under the new ones.

The one ask to remember: if you ever reset a forgotten passphrase while an anchor exists, the door asks you to type your twelve words once, so the new passphrase covers your old copies too. Decline, and the anchor simply keeps answering to the words.

What got simpler

Each of these was an open question with a long, conditional answer. The boundary rule closed them.

"Which mode is this library in?" derived from five separate inputs, each changed as a side effect by something else; one ambiguous state could record the wrong mode. written in the record — and structurally, the mode is just the kind of the current key. A key is born Castle or Standard and never changes kind, so there is nothing to derive.
"Which key file and Keychain slot count?" several key files per folder with newest-modified winning, and one Keychain slot shared by every copy of a library — restoring a backup silently overwrote the live key. one key file; one item per key. A restore never evicts anything, and opening a copy can never lower the mode.
"What opens an old backup?" each copy kept only its own day's keys; a library with no passphrase could lose everything with the Mac. the history: whatever opens your library opens its whole past, nothing more typed.
"What does leaving Castle expose?" three rulings in a row wrestled with which eras re-open to Apple, and the answer needed era bookkeeping on every key. the anchor: out of Castle, nothing crosses; back in, everything folds back. One object instead of a filtering rule.
"What does AirDropping a library share?" both Macs shared one key and its whole history forever — a standing exception, a hand-off ledger, caveats at Send Library, and the round-2 reviews' worst finding. a landing mints a fresh key with an empty history. A sent copy is just a copy; no two Macs ever share a key, and the exception, the ledger and the caveats are gone.
"Can the account I left still read my library?" yes, silently — the old account's master kept opening the library's current key through old backups, including everything written after leaving. leaving re-keys in both modes: the old account opens copies from while it synced, never anything after.
"Which Apple key wraps what?" one shared Apple key for every library and every account, never deleted — the root of Castle's gap, the "delete your old backups?" offer, and a special case for devices joining old accounts. one key per Mac per library, one per account for the vault's master only. Entering Castle deletes exactly this library's keys; the special case no longer exists.
"Is a half-finished switch dangerous?" crash markers lived inside the library file, rode into backups, and one stuck marker could refuse sync forever. sealed plans named by the record; a missing plan never blocks; copies pause around the swap, so every backup is whole.
"Can something on the Mac flip my protection?" the gate style, the idle lock and the scheduled export sat where any login-password holder could edit them, and pre-unlock code decided from what it found on disk. before unlock, nothing decides — the lobby offers only roads that prove themselves by opening the file. Weakening settings live in two sealed homes, newer wins; no repair ever makes a Touch ID item; the plaintext export turns off on the way into Castle.
"Who owns new writing?" whoever happened to be signed in stamped the rows, and "enrolled" had five meanings. the record names the account; writing belongs to the library's account. Signing in or out changes nothing.

The three honest sentences

What remains isn't complexity — it's truth the design states instead of hiding.

Leaving Castle opens the library you keep. Everything you wrote in Castle is still in it. The anchor protects your old copies — nothing can make the live library private again except going back into Castle.
A copy's locks are frozen the day it's made. Your current passphrase and words follow the library and the anchor — but a copy left behind in an era whose secrets you discarded answers only to the secrets of its day. Every copy says what opens it, in one line, wherever it's listed.
Variorum can't see iCloud Keychain. Whether the Apple road actually reached iCloud is Apple's to know. The passphrase and the words are the durable roads, in both modes.
