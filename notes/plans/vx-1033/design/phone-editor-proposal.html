The short version

Keep one page. Flip who is in charge of it.

The phone should stay one continuous page of text that you can select and edit across paragraphs. I agree that anything less is not a writing app, so the one-block-at-a-time design I floated earlier (option B below) is out.

Your read is half right. The phone is trying to do the Mac's trick without the thing that makes the trick work, and a fully custom text input is the only way to get that thing on an iPhone. But the Mac's trick is a means, not the feel itself. There is a second way to get the same feel that needs no custom input: let the text view behave like any text view, and make the blocks agree with the page afterwards. That is option D, and it is what I recommend.

  • Now: the safety fixes to today's editor (VM-252, VM-250), so your writing is safe while the new core is built.
  • Beside it: a new phone editor core on the "page is the truth" idea, behind a switch on your phone, with today's editor one flip away.
  • If iOS won't cooperate: a custom text input (option E) goes underneath the same core. Nothing built for D is thrown away.
  • The Mac's editor does not change in any option on this page.

One 15-minute session on your phone could change this recommendation. It is the first step either way.

The bar: what a great phone editor has to do

Every option below is judged against these six.

1 · It is one page

Tap anywhere and type. Select across paragraphs, cut, copy, paste, type over a selection, drag the handles as far as you like.

2 · The keyboard just works

Autocorrect, predictions, dictation, swipe typing, text replacements. None of them should ever cause a flicker, a jump or a lost letter.

3 · Every word lands in its block

What you type is saved into the block you put it in. Blocks join or split only when you mean it.

4 · Nothing is lost or saved wrong

When something goes wrong, the worst case is "nothing happened", and the app says so. Never silent damage.

5 · It stays fast

A keystroke costs the same in a ten-thousand-word note as in an empty one.

6 · The Mac stays as it is

“i like where the mac is at and I'm wary of changing it to accomodate the phone.”

Why the Mac's way works on the Mac and breaks on the phone

Both editors show the whole note in one text view and keep a private map of which characters belong to which block. The difference is who is allowed to change the text, and when the editor hears about it.

On the Mac: permission first
1You press a key, paste, or accept a correction.
→
2The text view asks the editor: may this change happen, and here?
→
3The editor approves it, already knowing the block. A change across two blocks is turned into a proper join first.
→
4The text changes. The map is updated by exactly that much.
→
5The block is saved.
On the iPhone today: find out afterwards
1You type. The keyboard also acts on its own: a grey prediction, an autocorrection, dictation's guesses.
→
2Some of those changes never ask. The editor learns of them from a report after the fact.
→
3If the report seems to cross two blocks, the editor refuses it and redraws the whole page from the library.
→
4The keyboard is still mid-word and does not know the page changed. Its next change lands at the old position.
→
5That misplaced change looks like yours, so it is saved.

What has been measured, on your phone and in tests

  • On your phone, ordinary keys and autocorrections did ask first (your trace of 23 August). Dictation's guesses did not.
  • A change made through the system's own "replace" road never asks: 24 of 24 in tests.
  • The report of what changed is sometimes stretched to the end of the paragraph. The very first notice is exact, and VM-24 now reads that one.
  • A newly typed character never carries our block mark. Characters already on the page keep theirs.
  • No test today types the way a real keyboard does. None covers predictions, dictation, system undo or Writing Tools.

Sources: notes/TEXTKIT2_NOTES.md §3 (the iOS 26 and 26.2 findings); the editor audit in notes/plans/vx-1033/phone-editor-audit.md; tickets VM-250 and VM-252.

Every fix so far (the snap-back, the keystroke fence, the faint text after a dash) closed one road. But the roads belong to the keyboard, not to us, and each iOS release adds more. A design that is only safe when we have listed every road is the real problem.

Is your read right?

Where you are right

The Mac's trick is permission: nothing changes until the editor has approved it and knows which block it belongs to. The phone copied that design, but an iPhone keyboard does not always ask.

A fully custom text input is the only way to get full permission on an iPhone. With one, the keyboard cannot touch the text except by calling our code. So yes: if the goal is "exactly the Mac's mechanism", only option E delivers it.

Where I'd push back

Permission is how the Mac gets the feel. It is not the feel. The feel is: one page, edits land where you make them, and blocks never get in your way.

There is a second way to get that, which needs no permission at all: let the text view be an ordinary text view, accept every change, and make the blocks agree with the page afterwards. An editor built that way does not care whether the keyboard asked. That is option D.

The five options

A and D keep one text view. B and C confine the keyboard to one block. E replaces Apple's text view with our own.

D

How it works

  • Blocks are marked on the page itself. Each block's start is recorded on its paragraph break. There is no separate map to fall out of step, so the "refuse and redraw" moment cannot happen.
  • Every change is accepted, then read. After any change, asked-about or not, the editor re-reads the few paragraphs around it and saves each block whose text changed. It never needs to know which kind of change it was.
  • The page is never redrawn from the library while the keyboard is up. A change from elsewhere (a sync, a take swap, a divide from the menu) is made as a small local edit, announced to the keyboard, and held back while the keyboard is mid-word. The paragraph you are typing in is never overwritten from outside; a conflict becomes a take, as today.
  • Structure follows the page. Return's new line gets a mark: that is a new block. Backspacing over a mark joins two blocks. Selecting across three paragraphs and typing replaces them, as in any editor, and the library records the join your existing rules define. A mark the keyboard damages by itself is put back.
  • Text the keyboard is still forming is not saved until it settles.
In your hand
A normal text editor. Select, cut, paste and type across paragraphs. Backspace joins. Predictions and autocorrect simply work, because nothing refuses them and nothing redraws under them.
Good at
  • The feel you asked for, with native selection, loupe, handles and accessibility, because it is still Apple's text view.
  • Being correct without a list of the keyboard's roads. "Any change at all leaves a page the editor reads correctly" becomes a test: thousands of random edits thrown at the core on the Mac, no phone needed.
  • A much smaller core. The approve-first machinery, the fence and the repair road are not needed.
  • Wave 3's Return and backspace become cheap and safe instead of risky.
What it costs
A new phone editor core, then moving today's features onto it: takes and the peek, the markdown look, badges and marks, the open end of a note, find. Built behind a switch in Settings ▸ Diagnostics, with today's editor one flip away until you retire it.
Problems we'd have
  • Keeping the keyboard in step when the page changes elsewhere. This is the one thing that could sink D. If the keyboard keeps writing at old positions even after being told the page changed, D cannot be made safe at structural moments, and only owning the input (E) can. It can be tested early, on your phone.
  • Knowing when predicted or dictated text has settled. If iOS does not say, we either turn off the grey inline prediction (the bar above the keyboard stays), or accept that a guess can be saved for a moment and corrected at the next key, never into a commit.
  • Telling your own cross-block edit from the keyboard's accident when a mark disappears. Your own edits ask first on your phone, which is how we would tell. A wrong call is never destructive: a join keeps both blocks in lineage, and a mark that was put back can be removed by doing it again.
  • System undo across a join or split (shake to undo). It needs a rule. The Mac's rule is to clear the text undo at that point.
  • Badges, callouts and comment marks still depend on where the text view lays lines out, as today (VM-17, VM-239). D does not fix that by itself.
Has to be proven
The measuring session now. Then a spike: D's core in the app behind the switch, and the random-edit test on the Mac.
Verdict: recommended. It gives the Mac's feel by the opposite mechanism, and it is the first half of E if E is ever needed.

What other apps do

From an Opus researcher's read of Apple's documentation, Apple engineers' forum answers and other editors' source code (22 November 2026). Each claim's source, date and confidence is in notes/plans/vx-1033/ios-text-input-research.md.

  • No native block editor on iPhone lets a selection run across blocks and be typed over. Anytype, which is open source and uses one text view per block, switches to whole-block selection when a drag passes a block's edge (shipped July 2026). Notion's cross-block selection exists only in its web-view editor. Craft's documented road is block selection. So option B would have put the phone in that company.
  • Selection across several text views is not supported by iOS (Apple support, August 2024). That is the wall option C leans against.
  • The "ask first" road is unreliable beyond what we measured. Several kinds of change never ask (the double-space period, some prediction taps, swipe typing in one report), and some that ask describe a different change from the one made. Apple lists the single-range "ask first" call as deprecated at iOS 27.
  • The grey inline prediction is text the keyboard is still forming. iOS hands it to the text view as "marked" text, and one setting turns it off for a single text view. What accepting and rejecting it write is not documented; your phone has to show us.
  • Changing text under the keyboard has a right door. An Apple engineer's advice (2023): never edit the text's storage directly; go through the text view's own insert and replace calls. React Native moved to this shape in October 2026: decide in the "ask" call, apply any correction as a small replace on the next turn, and never while text is being composed. That is option D's rule for changing the page from outside.
  • System replacements can drop a custom mark from the characters they replace, while keeping font and colour. D has to check its marks after every change and repair them, never trust them blindly. The plan does.
  • A custom text input has a long tail in practice. Runestone, a shipping custom editor, was still adding basics in late 2026: spelling underlines built by hand, Writing Tools support, fixes for composing input. In June 2026 an Apple engineer recommended a stock text view for a new iOS 27 editor.
  • iOS 27 lets a text view draw its own surfaces beside the text. That could make badges and marks beside a block easier in one text view (VM-17, VM-239), which this page had counted as a weakness of D.

Should the phone stop making blocks on new lines?

“should the phone default to not making new blocks on new lines? not being able to drag and edit across a new line is frustrating.”

No. It would not fix this, and it would cost you what blocks are for.

It would not fix it

Most of your notes already have a block for each paragraph, made on the Mac. The phone has to edit across those boundaries whatever it does with new text.

It would cost

Takes are per block. If the phone wrote one big block, a paragraph written there could not have its own takes until you divided it, and the same note would be shaped differently depending on where you typed it. Today this is one library-wide setting, on purpose.

What is actually frustrating

Today the phone refuses backspace at the start of a block, with “This keystroke can't join these two blocks. Merge the blocks deliberately instead…”. The Mac joins. That refusal is on screen at the very start of your recording.

Under D that wall is gone: backspace joins, and a selection across the line just works. Under A it goes away in wave 3, at more risk.

The call that stays yours

What Return does on the phone. You ruled "a new block, as on the Mac". D makes either answer cheap. I would keep your ruling.

The path I recommend

Each step is useful by itself, and each one tells us whether the next is needed.

Step 0 · now

Measure on your phone

Fifteen minutes with the edit trace on, on the build you already have. It settles which keyboard changes ask first and whether the grey guess is real text on the page.
Step 1 · this wave

Make today's editor safe

The latch for VM-252, a page-and-map check in the shipped app, no redraw under text still forming. Your writing is safe while the rest is built.
Step 2 · this wave, beside step 1

Build D's core behind a switch

Settings ▸ Diagnostics ▸ a switch for the new editor, on your real notes. Plus the random-edit test results from the Mac.
The gate · yours

Real writing on the new core

Pass: no lost letter, no jump, selection and backspace across paragraphs feel right, and the keyboard stays in step when the page changes elsewhere.
Step 3 · after the gate

Move everything onto it

Takes, marks, the markdown look, the note's open end. Then wave 3's Writing is built on it, and the old editor is retired at your word.

If the gate fails on "keyboard in step"

Build our own input layer under D's core. That is option E, started with its harder half already done.

If the gate fails on feel somewhere else

Stay on today's editor and run A's second half: gentler redraws, caret handling, proposals never becoming structural acts.

What I am sure of, and what I am not

Sure

  • The diagnosis. It rests on your recording, two audits and my own reading of the code.
  • B and C cannot give editing across paragraphs.
  • D's core idea can be tested thoroughly without a phone.
  • None of the five touches the Mac's editor.
  • The safety fixes in step 1 are worth doing whichever option you pick.

Not sure

  • Whether iOS keeps the keyboard in step when we announce a change elsewhere on the page. This decides D versus E.
  • Whether the grey inline guess is real text on the page, and whether iOS says when it has settled.
  • What system undo does across a join.
  • How long E's tail of feel problems really is.
  • Whether D feels right in your hand. I have reasoned about it; nobody has held it.
