The Keeper's Hand

The lighthouse keeper's logs survive in a glass case at the maritime museum, forty years of weather in a hand that never hurries. Wind NNE, moderate. Glass falling. The lamp trimmed at dusk.

What strikes you first is not what he recorded but what he refused to. No storm is dramatized. No shipwreck gets more than its coordinates and a time. The sea that took nine men in October 1911 receives the same margin as a Tuesday of fog.

I used to think this restraint was a failure of feeling — that a man who could watch a schooner break apart and write four lines had gone numb from the isolation. Now I think the log was the only place he could put the sea down and leave it there.⑂ 3

I used to read this restraint as numbness. I no longer do. The log was where he set the sea down each night — and the setting down was the point.
take 3
It would be easy to call the restraint numbness, the flat hand of a man alone too long with weather. But keepers were replaced when they went strange, and he kept forty years. The plainness was a discipline, the way a rope is coiled the same whether or not anyone drowned that day.

My own journal does the opposite of everything his did. It dramatizes the fog. It editorializes the Tuesday. And it would not survive forty days of rereading, let alone forty years.

Maybe that is the real use of keeping any log at all: not to remember the weather, but to practice the hand that can hold it.

Logbooks older version

At the maritime museum there is a glass case of logbooks, and I keep going back to it. Forty years of one man's weather. The handwriting never speeds up.

Nine men drowned within sight of his lamp in 1911 and the entry runs three lines. I copied it into my notebook and my copy is longer than his original, because I couldn't stop myself from commenting.

That is the whole difference between us, I think. He wrote to put the day down. I write to keep the day going.

I would like to learn the other thing. Not the plainness itself — the being able to afford it.
