The Keeper's Hand

The lighthouse keeper's logs survive in a glass case at the maritime museum, forty years of weather in a hand that never hurries. Wind NNE, moderate. Glass falling. The lamp trimmed at dusk.

What strikes you first is not what he recorded but what he refused to. No storm is dramatized. No shipwreck gets more than its coordinates and a time. The sea that took nine men in October 1911 receives the same margin as a Tuesday of fog.

…⑂ 5

My own journal does the opposite of everything his did. It dramatizes the fog. It editorializes the Tuesday. And it would not survive forty days of rereading, let alone forty years.

Maybe that is the real use of keeping any log at all: not to remember the weather, but to practice the hand that can hold it.

What strikes you first is not what he recorded but what he refused to. No storm is dramatized. No shipwreck gets more than its coordinates and a time. The sea that took nine men in October 1911 receives the same margin as a Tuesday of fog.
I used to think this restraint was a failure of feeling — that a man who could watch a schooner break apart and write four lines had gone numb from the isolation. Now I think the log was the only place he could put the sea down and leave it there.
That is the whole difference between us, I think. He wrote to put the day down. I write to keep the day going.
My own journal does the opposite of everything his did. It dramatizes the fog. It editorializes the Tuesday. And it would not survive forty days of rereading, let alone forty years.
I would like to learn the other thing. Not the plainness itself — the being able to afford it.
Maybe that is the real use of keeping any log at all: not to remember the weather, but to practice the hand that can hold it.
